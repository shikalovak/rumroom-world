Planning your first trip to **Bali**? This is one of those places that quietly stays with you long after you leave. This guide covers what most first-timers wish they'd known before their first trip: which neighborhoods actually fit which kind of trip, when to come (and when to skip), what's worth doing versus pure Instagram bait, the visa basics, how to move around without losing your mind, and the rookie mistakes nearly everyone makes the first time. No fluff — just practical, straightforward advice.

## Quick hits

- **Best time:** June–August or September (dry season, manageable crowds)
- **Where to stay:** Canggu for action, Pererenan for balance, Uluwatu for cliffs & sunsets, Ubud for nature retreats
- **Visa:** Visa on arrival (30 days, around $30, extendable once for another 30 days) or e-visa; straightforward for most Western passport holders
- **Entry fee:** A Bali tourist levy of IDR 150,000 (about $9–10) applies to all foreign visitors — pay it online via the official Love Bali portal before you fly, or at the airport
- **Transport:** Scooter is common; Grab for taxis; car transfers if you want to relax
- **Budget:** $800–1,500/month for decent housing; food & activities cheap; scooter rentals ~$5–8/day

> 💱 *All prices in USD. EU readers, rough conversion: 1 USD ≈ €0.92.*

## Why Bali is such a good first trip to Southeast Asia

Let's be honest: half the world heard about Bali from *Eat Pray Love*. The healers, the rice fields, the spiritual reset — Elizabeth Gilbert's Ubud chapter put this island on the global map in 2010, and the wave never really stopped. Today Bali is one of the most visited islands on the planet, and that has consequences both ways: amazing infrastructure, English everywhere, every cuisine you want — but also crowds in the obvious spots and a tourism economy that can feel intense if you don't know where to go.

Here's what makes it work *despite* the hype:

✈️ **Direct and connecting flights** from North America and Europe (via Tokyo, Seoul, Hong Kong)
🛂 **Straightforward tourist visa** — no bureaucratic surprise
🧘 **Built-out infrastructure** — yoga studios, coworking spaces, gyms, hospitals, restaurants
🏥 **Good, affordable healthcare** — private clinics with English-speaking staff
🏡 **Housing for every budget** — $5 dorm beds to $300/night villas with infinity pools
🌊 **You can actually blend everything** — work, beach days, social scene, rice terraces, temples, all within an hour or two

Bali feels less like "raw Southeast Asia" and more like a successful hybrid: local culture plus tourist-friendly comfort. That's rare. Even on your first trip, you won't feel lost — scooter rentals are easy, food delivery apps work, and people speak enough English to help you out. For anyone who's never been to Asia before, this is a soft landing.

## When to go: seasons and what to actually expect

The island's open year-round, but the seasons feel *very* different:

**January–February: Wet season** 🌧️
Heavy rain, usually short bursts, but it's frequent and humidity is high. Clothes take forever to dry. The ocean gets murky with debris; surfers have a harder time. The vibe can feel cozy if you like empty beaches, but it's uncomfortable for a first trip—the dampness gets into everything.

**March–April: Shoulder season**
Rain dials back but isn't gone. Fresh greenery, fewer tourists, lower prices. It's a solid pick if you don't mind some unpredictability and want to save money.

**May–June: Dry season begins** 🌿
Hot but not suffocating. Ocean is clear. Low humidity. One of the better windows for a first visit.

**July–August: Peak dry season** ☀️
Stable weather: 82–86°F (28–30°C) during the day, around 75°F (24°C) at night. Warm ocean, good surf, and yes—*crowds*. Lots of them, especially in Canggu.

**September–October: Still dry, fewer people** 🍃
September is often considered one of the best months: still fresh, ocean's clean, and the tourist crush hasn't fully arrived. Prices tend to drop too.

**November–December: Rain returns**
Short downpours at first, then longer rain spells. Humidity climbs, mosquitoes multiply. (December's New Year on the beach is popular, though 🎉)

**Best overall bet:** June–August or September. You'll get good weather without the worst of the crowds or heat.

## Where to stay: neighborhoods and honest takes

Bali isn't one city—it's a collection of totally different vibes. Pick wrong, and you might have a rougher trip than expected.

**Canggu: The epicenter** 📍
Markets, endless cafes, ocean, surfing, and *people*. Constant motion. The upside: you're never bored. The downside: traffic is heavy, it's crowded, and it can feel intense compared to a quieter beach town. Great for a short vacation or if you're drawn to nightlife and coworking. Not ideal if you want to actually feel like you've left somewhere stressful.

**Pererenan: The sweet spot** 📍
Close to Canggu's action but more spacious and calm. Still some rice fields, fresher air. For a first time in Bali, this is a solid base — you get the essentials without the sensory overload. [More about where to stay in Bali](/bali/where-to-stay/)

**Uluwatu: Cliffs and sunsets** 📍
Dramatic ocean views, surf breaks, incredible sunsets. Real downside: everything's spread out, so you're either scootering constantly or paying for transfers. But if you want raw beauty and ocean time, it's unbeatable.

**Ubud: Jungle and spiritual retreats** 📍
Rice terraces, temples, yoga, and ceremonies. Perfect if you're after yoga retreats and a nature reset. It can feel quiet compared to the coast (and the ocean's far), but for anyone seeking that spiritual element, it's a strong choice.

**Kuta: Consider skipping it as a base** 📍
It has a busy, nightlife-heavy energy — fine for a surf lesson, but most first-timers do better basing themselves elsewhere. For now: consider it a stopover rather than a base.

**Seminyak: The boutique version** 📍
Stylish restaurants, rooftop bars, beach clubs, designer shops. Less low-key than Canggu, closer to Kuta's energy but generally cleaner and calmer. Lots of dining and accommodation options.

## Getting from the airport to your place

Denpasar Airport (DPS) is on the south side of the island. Drive time depends entirely on traffic.

**Grab or Gojek taxi:** Best option. Use the app, not the taxi stand — drivers at the stand often quote well above the app price.
**Scooter rental:** Only if you're confident driving one. For first-timers, better to wait.
**Private transfer:** Smooth but pricier.

**Rough times from airport:**
Canggu: 40–70 minutes
Seminyak: 30–40 minutes
Uluwatu: 45–60 minutes
Ubud: 1.5–2 hours

**Pro move:** Arrive during the day if you can. Night driving with jet lag is rough.

## Finding housing: short-term vs. long-term

There's a place for every budget—$5 dorm beds to $300+/night private villas.

**For a week or two:**
Airbnb and Booking work fine. Book online.

**For a month or longer:**
Facebook groups (search "Bali Housing" or check expat groups), Telegram channels, or walk around looking for "For Rent" signs. The last option is underrated—prices are sometimes lower when you bypass apps and deal directly with an owner.

**Rough monthly costs (for two people):**
Guesthouse: $400–800
Apartment: $800–1,500
Villa with pool: $1,500–2,000+

**Practical advice:** Book your first week online for peace of mind. Then, after you arrive and get a feel for which area actually suits you, look for a place in person. You can often save money and avoid committing to the wrong area. [Detailed guide to where to live in Bali](/bali/where-to-live/)

## Getting around: scooters, taxis, and common sense

**Scooters** 🛵
This is a common way people get around. Rentals run $5–8/day. If you're confident riding, it's freedom. If you're not, don't force it — falling on pavement at speed hurts, and Bali's roads have real hazards.

**Grab/Gojek** 🚕
Ride-sharing apps work well for when you don't want to ride a scooter. Generally cheap and reliable.

**Rented car with driver** 🚙
For longer trips or if you're tired, hiring a car with a driver for the day is worth the extra cost for comfort.

**The reality:** Scooter culture is prominent here, and traffic can feel chaotic by Western standards. Roads are narrow, signage is minimal, and local drivers move fast. [Full guide to scooter rental in Bali](/bali/scooter-rental/)

## Visa and money: what you need to know

**Visa for most Western passport holders** 🛂
You can get a **Visa on Arrival (VOA)** at the airport — around 500,000 IDR (roughly $30), valid 30 days. It can be extended once for another 30 days, for a maximum stay of about 60 days; as of mid-2025, extensions generally require an in-person visit to an immigration office. There's also an **e-VOA option** you can apply for online before you fly, which can speed up arrival. Rules and fees change from time to time, so confirm current details on the [official Indonesia e-Visa site](https://evisa.imigrasi.go.id/) before you travel.

**Bali tourist levy** 💵
Since February 2024, all foreign visitors entering Bali for tourism pay a one-time levy of IDR 150,000 (about $9–10). Pay it online in advance via the official Love Bali portal, or at counters on arrival — advance payment avoids the queue.

**Currency and cards** 💵
Indonesian Rupiah (IDR) is the local currency. ATMs are widely available. Cards work at most places, but keep cash on hand — many local restaurants and shops are cash-only.

**Daily costs (rough estimates):**
Meal at a warung (local restaurant): $2–4
Nice dinner: $10–20
Beer: $1.50–3
Scooter rental: $5–8/day
Travel insurance: Check SafetyWing or local options

For longer stays, consider an **eSIM** (like Airalo) for data, or get a local SIM at the airport.

## How long should you stay? (Real itineraries that work)

The biggest first-timer mistake is treating Bali like a European city break — three days, four spots a day, done. That's a good way to leave with sore feet and little real feel for the place. Here's a reasonable way to plan it depending on your time:

🏝 **3 days in Bali** — the essentials sampler
South coast only (Canggu + Pererenan beaches), one Uluwatu sunset, a beginner surf lesson, and one slow morning at a cafe. Skip Ubud — it's hard to do justice in 3 days. → [Read the full 3-day Bali itinerary](/bali/itinerary-3-days/)

🌴 **7 days in Bali** — first taste of variety
Add 2 nights in Ubud for rice terraces, a temple visit, and one waterfall. Keep the rest on the south coast. → [Read the full 7-day Bali itinerary](/bali/itinerary-7-days/)

🌺 **14 days in Bali** — actually feeling the island
Rotate through 3 neighborhoods (Canggu/Pererenan, Ubud, Uluwatu — or swap Uluwatu for Nusa Penida). Mix beach time with one jungle retreat day, one inland village, and some slower mornings. Don't over-schedule. → [Read the full 14-day Bali itinerary](/bali/itinerary-14-days/)

**Real talk:** Bali isn't a marathon. It's about *feeling* the place, not checking boxes. Roads take longer than you think, and one temple visit can easily be a half-day thing. Do less, absorb more.

## Weather and what to actually wear

Bali doesn't have winter in the way you might be used to. It's warm year-round, but the *feeling* varies wildly.

**Dry season (June–August):**
Daytime: 82–86°F (28–30°C)
Nighttime: 75°F (24°C)
Ocean: 81°F (27°C)
Vibe: Comfortable, breezy. Ocean wind makes the heat bearable.

**Wet season (Nov–Feb):**
Daytime: 84–88°F (29–31°C)
Humidity: High. *Really* high.
Vibe: Everything feels damp. Clothes don't dry easily. It's less the heat and more the moisture.

**In the mountains (Ubud, Uluwatu):**
Can actually feel cool at night. A light hoodie isn't overkill.

**About sunburn:** Don't underestimate it. Even when it's cloudy, you can burn in around 30 minutes. SPF 50+ is non-negotiable.

## Rookie mistakes (and how to avoid them)

🚫 **Renting a car instead of riding a scooter**
Cars mean traffic, parking headaches, and being stuck on narrow island roads. A scooter offers more freedom, but only ride if you're confident.

🚫 **Booking accommodations at the last minute in high season**
Good villas get booked out weeks in advance. Book early for July–August.

🚫 **Overpacking your itinerary**
This isn't Europe, where you can sprint through several sights in a day. Roads take time. One place can easily be a half-day thing. Plan loose.

🚫 **Ignoring sun and humidity**
Sunburn happens fast, even through clouds. Humidity means moisture everywhere (clothes, bags, electronics). Bring fast-dry clothes.

🚫 **Not getting travel insurance**
Scooter accidents happen. Stray dogs exist. Infections from cuts are a real risk. Clinics are generally good but expensive without coverage. Travel insurance is comparatively cheap; medical bills aren't.

🚫 **Overpacking clothes**
You'll mostly wear swimsuits, light shirts, and maybe one hoodie for evenings. Everything else tends to be "just in case" weight.

🚫 **Heading straight to Kuta or Ubud expecting the "real" experience**
For a first trip, Canggu, Pererenan, or Uluwatu tend to give easier access to everything without some of the downsides.

## Putting it all together: a suggested approach

If you're planning a first trip to Bali, here's a reasonable way to structure it:

1. **Book the first week in Pererenan or South Canggu** — close enough to the action, far enough from the busiest streets.
2. **Consider a scooter after a day or two if you feel confident, otherwise stick with Grab** — there's no downside to starting cautious.
3. **Plan one day trip to Ubud and one to Uluwatu**, then leave the rest flexible.
4. **Aim for June or September** — the weather tends to be good, and the island feels less overrun.
5. **Stay longer than a week if possible** — Bali tends to reveal itself slowly. A week works, but two weeks gives more room to actually settle in.

Bottom line: Bali is a genuinely solid choice for a first trip to Southeast Asia. It's comfortable enough that you're unlikely to feel overwhelmed, varied enough that you won't be bored, and distinctive enough that it's easy to connect with.

## FAQ: Your actual questions, answered

**Q: Can I scooter with just an international driver's license?**
A: Technically, you need an International Driving Permit (IDP) alongside your regular license. In practice, rental shops don't always check closely. But if you're stopped or in an accident without one, you could face trouble with police or insurance. Get the IDP before you go, or use Grab instead.

**Q: Is it safe to ride a scooter here?**
A: It carries more risk than in many home countries. Local riders move fast, and road rules aren't always closely followed. Wear a helmet (legally required), stick to main roads at first, and assume other traffic may be moving faster than expected. Avoid riding at night if you're new to it.

**Q: What's the actual cost of living?**
A: Budget travel: roughly $30–50/day. Comfortable mid-range: roughly $60–100/day. Nice meals and activities: $100–150+/day. Generally cheaper than the US, though not as cheap as Thailand or Cambodia.

**Q: Should I book a tour or go solo?**
A: Both work. Ubud temple tours and waterfall hikes often benefit from a guide. Beach days and restaurants are fine solo. Consider a [scooter rental in Bali](/bali/scooter-rental/) or Grab for flexibility.

**Q: Best travel insurance for Bali?**
A: SafetyWing is a common choice for backpackers and short trips. For longer stays (1+ month), compare it against AXA or local Indonesian options. Make sure whatever you choose covers scooter accidents and confirm what it says about medical evacuation.

**Q: Do I need vaccinations?**
A: Check with your doctor, but Bali's medical facilities are generally good and most travelers are fine with standard vaccines up to date. Dengue fever is present in Bali — long sleeves, repellent, and covered spaces at dusk help reduce risk.

If you only do five things:
- Check flight prices for June–September
- Book the first 5–7 nights, then leave the rest open

Bali tends to open up when you stop trying to see everything at once.
