Two weeks in Italy sounds like plenty of time. It's actually not — if you're planning to sleep in a different city every other night. I did the classic Rome-Florence-Venice circuit on my first Italian trip and I remember almost none of it because I spent half the time collecting luggage and the other half apologizing to hotel staff for arriving late.

**Here's the real truth:** you need fewer bases, more nights in one place, and a clear principle for how you move. This post gives you three honest routes, each tied to one core idea: trains only, car and countryside, or pure south and slow.

---

## Route 1: Rome, Naples, Amalfi — Trains and Ferries Only

**Days 1–5: Rome.** You're landing in Rome, and you need at least four full days there. I know that sounds like a lot if you're a monument-chaser, but honestly, sitting in a piazza at 10 PM with a €3 Peroni *is* Rome. You're also jet-lagged; pretend it's not an issue and that's when you hit the Roman Forum in midday heat and resent every stone.

Stay in Testaccio or Trastevere if you have a budget above €90/night. Below that, look further out but still on metro lines. Check [Booking.com](https://www.booking.com/searchresults.html?ss=Italy) a few weeks ahead — €60–100 per night is realistic for a decent room, €120–150 if you want something with morning light and a decent bathroom.

**Days 6–8: Naples.** Rome to Naples is a 1-hour 10-minute Frecciarossa ride (€19–45 if booked early, €50–80 walk-up). You're in the real south now. Naples is chaos and loud and absolutely worth it. Stay in the centro storico if you can find it under €80/night, or in Chiaia a bit further out. Three nights lets you breathe — walk the old city, eat pizza twice, visit Pompeii as a day trip.

Train from Naples to Salerno (the Amalfi gateway) is 35–60 minutes by regionale, around €8–12. Not glamorous but it works.

**Days 9–14: Amalfi Coast.** Six nights on the coast. This is where your trip becomes an actual memory instead of a camera roll. Pick one base — Salerno is chaotic-fun and cheaper, Positano is picture-postcard and €200+/night, Ravello is quiet and in the hills. Honestly, Salerno is the move. You'll save €400+ and spend less time on a scooter.

The SITA bus network runs along the coast. Ferries (seasonal, roughly April–October, 35 minutes to Capri) are the bonus for having time.

### This route costs (per person):
- **Accommodation:** €60–100/night (Rome, Naples, Salerno mixed) × 14 nights = **€840–1400**
- **Train tickets:** Rome–Naples, Naples–Salerno, local trains = **€50–80**
- **Food & coffee:** If you eat pizza, pasta, and pasta again, roughly **€400–600** (one nice dinner included)
- **Activities:** Pompeii entry (~€13), boats, museums roughly **€150–250**
- **Total:** **€1440–2330 per person**

Real take: this route is the goldilocks if you're not driving. You see three distinct regions of Italy (central, south, coast) with almost zero stress about navigation. The train system just works, even if it's sometimes late.

## Route 2: Florence and Tuscany — Car from Day Four

**Days 1–3: Rome.** Same as above. Three nights, Testaccio or Trastevere, jet lag is a feature not a bug.

**Day 4: Rome to Florence by train.** One hour 30 minutes, then rent a car for the next 10 days.

**Days 4–14: Tuscany.** Base yourself in Florence for 3 nights, then pick a Tuscan town as your car hub. Montepulciano or Montalcino are both real towns with actual residents, not Airbnb open-air museums.

From Florence, it's 2–2.5 hours to either. From there, you have day-trip range to:
- **Pienza** (30 minutes) — Renaissance town, great for lunch
- **Crete Senesi** (1 hour) — those rolling clay hills everyone photographs
- **Montepulciano to Pienza** loop (doable as one long drive)
- **Chianti villages** — west of Montalcino, wineries, cypress roads

I'd actually do is spend 4 nights in Florence, then 7 nights in Montalcino or Montepulciano, one town only. The car stress of changing bases every two nights isn't worth the "I've been to more places" feeling.

**Important:** ZTL (limited-traffic zones) exist in Florence, Siena, and every Tuscan town center. If you get caught on camera (you'll get a fine 2–3 months later via the rental company), add €80 admin fee on top. Park outside the centro storico and walk.

### This route costs (per person):
- **Accommodation:** €60–80/night in Rome/Florence, €50–70/night in smaller towns × 14 nights = **€770–1120**
- **Train:** Rome–Florence = **€20–50**
- **Car rental:** 10 days, mid-size automatic, ~€40–55/day = **€400–550**
- **Gas:** ~€40–60 for 10 days of local driving
- **Parking:** €15–25 total across towns
- **Food & coffee:** Pizza and pasta, roughly **€450–650** (one winery lunch counts)
- **Activities:** Galleries, wine tastings, roughly **€200–350**
- **Total:** **€1890–2780 per person**

My take: you'll drive an average of 1.5–2 hours per day and spend 4–5 hours not moving. That's the pacing that actually sticks. You see fewer places but you actually see them.

## Route 3: Puglia — Slowest, Cheapest, Least Crowded

**Days 1–4: Rome to Bari by train, then south.** Rome to Bari on Frecciarossa is 4 hours, €30–80 depending on book timing. No connection needed; you're flying south.

**Days 4–14: Bari → Lecce → coast, car required.**

Puglia doesn't have a real train network for what you want to see. You need a car here. Rent for 10 days in Bari (€40–50/day, same ZTL rules as Tuscany apply).

Base yourself 3 nights in Lecce (the gorgeous Baroque town, 1.5 hours from Bari), then 4 nights on the Adriatic coast in Otranto or Polignano a Mare, then 3 nights back to Bari area to catch your flight out.

From Lecce, day trips are:
- **Otranto** (45 minutes) — cliff town, castle, pebble beach
- **Gallipoli** (1 hour) — lagoon-side fishing village
- **Santa Maria di Leuca** (1.5 hours) — southern tip, just vibes

This region genuinely has fewer tourists. If I were planning this from scratch in August (when I wouldn't), Puglia would be my only choice because the crowds move north to Venice and Tuscany.

### This route costs (per person):
- **Accommodation:** €40–65/night (Puglia is cheaper) × 14 nights = **€560–910**
- **Train:** Rome–Bari = **€35–70**
- **Car rental:** 10 days × €40–50 = **€400–500**
- **Gas:** €30–50
- **Parking:** €20 total (way cheaper than Tuscany)
- **Food:** Orecchiette, fresh seafood, €300–450 (you eat *well* cheaply here)
- **Activities:** Minimal entry fees, mostly coastal walking, **€80–150**
- **Total:** **€1505–2180 per person**

Honestly, this is the budget win. You get fewer crowds, lower prices, and a genuinely different side of Italy. The tradeoff is you spend more time driving and less time in the conversation about Italy that happens in Florence.

## The One-Move Rule: Change Your Base No More Than Three Times

**Honestly,** every time you pack a suitcase in Italy, you lose 90 minutes to packing, 45 minutes to traveling, and another hour getting oriented. That's nearly three hours of your day gone.

Two weeks = 14 days. If you're moving bases every two nights, you're moving 7 times. That's 21 hours of lost daylight just to transit and settling.

Here's the rule: **change base no more than three times.** That means:
- **Rome** (nights 1–5)
- **Tuscany or coast** (nights 6–11)
- **One final place** (nights 12–14) OR just stay put in night 2 location

Your days have breathing room. You actually know where the good coffee spot is by day three. You don't drag a suitcase up five flights of stairs twice in one trip.

All three routes above follow this rule. Do it.

## What to Book Ahead, What to Leave Open

**Book these:**
- **Accommodation** — 6–8 weeks out. July and August are €50+ more per night. April, May, September, October are sweet spots.
- **Trains** — 2–4 weeks ahead for Frecciarossa, you'll save €20–40 per ticket. Regional trains (Naples–Salerno) don't need booking; they run every 30 minutes.
- **Rental car** — 2–3 weeks out. Collision damage waivers and coverage details matter; read the small print. (affiliate link coming soon for comparison sites)

**Leave open:**
- **Museums** — Skip the long advance lines. Museums are never actually full except in August. Walk up, pay at the door.
- **Restaurant reservations** — Italians eat late (9–10 PM). If you want dinner at 7 PM, anywhere will have a table. Treat it like real life, not a puzzle.
- **Day trips** — If the weather is bad, change plans. If you meet someone interesting, follow them for an hour. The itinerary is a skeleton, not a script.

## My Honest Take

Here's the thing nobody wants to hear: two weeks is not enough to "really know Italy." It's enough to know one region well and to have actual memories instead of Instagram photos.

If this is your first trip, pick one route and stick with it. Don't try to see the north and south in one go. I see travelers trying to do Rome–Florence–Venice–Amalfi in 14 days, and they look exhausted by day eight. They've seen 15 things poorly instead of 5 things deeply.

**Real take:** the best Italy trip is the one where you spend three days in one town and actually recognize the owner of the coffee bar on the last morning. You miss that in an itinerary that looks impressive on paper.

August is brutally hot and expensive and also when Italians close their businesses to take a month off. Avoid it if you can. September is better. Spring (May, early June) is the actual best.

And here's the move I'd actually make if I were planning from scratch: pick Route 1 or Route 3 (not Route 2, Tuscany is everywhere). Spend a week south of Rome. Spend your second week in one of those towns, getting slow and a bit bored and a little bit local. That's Italy.
