Two weeks in Italy sounds like plenty of time. It's actually not, if the plan is to sleep in a different city every other night. The classic Rome–Florence–Venice circuit, packed into two weeks with constant moving, tends to leave travelers with more memories of luggage logistics and rushed check-ins than of the destinations themselves.

**Here's the practical takeaway:** fewer bases, more nights in one place, and a clear principle for how to move. This post lays out three routes, each built around one core idea: trains only, car and countryside, or pure south and slow.

---

## Route 1: Rome, Naples, Amalfi — Trains and Ferries Only

**Days 1–5: Rome.** Landing in Rome calls for at least four full days there. That might sound like a lot for a monument-focused visitor, but sitting in a piazza at 10 PM with a €3 Peroni is as much "Rome" as any museum. Jet lag is also a real factor early on — hitting the Roman Forum in midday heat straight off a flight tends to be more draining than it looks on paper.

Testaccio or Trastevere work well for a budget above €90/night; below that, look slightly further out but still on metro lines. [Booking.com](https://www.booking.com/searchresults.html?ss=Italy) a few weeks ahead typically shows €60–100 per night for a decent room, €120–150 for something with better light and a nicer bathroom.

**Days 6–8: Naples.** Rome to Naples is a roughly 1-hour-10-minute Frecciarossa ride (€19–45 booked early, €50–80 walk-up). Naples is loud, chaotic, and generally considered worth it. The centro storico under €80/night or Chiaia slightly further out both work as bases. Three nights allows time to walk the old city, eat pizza more than once, and visit Pompeii as a day trip.

The train from Naples to Salerno (the Amalfi gateway) runs 35–60 minutes by regionale, around €8–12.

**Days 9–14: Amalfi Coast.** Six nights on the coast turns the trip from a series of sights into something more settled. Salerno is livelier and cheaper, Positano is picture-postcard and €200+/night, Ravello is quiet and set in the hills. Salerno tends to be the more budget-friendly choice, saving several hundred euros over a Positano base while requiring less reliance on scooters or taxis to get around.

The SITA bus network runs along the coast. Ferries (seasonal, roughly April–October, about 35 minutes to Capri) are a good option when time allows.

### This route costs (per person):
- **Accommodation:** €60–100/night (Rome, Naples, Salerno mixed) × 14 nights = **€840–1400**
- **Train tickets:** Rome–Naples, Naples–Salerno, local trains = **€50–80**
- **Food & coffee:** roughly **€400–600** for regular meals plus one nicer dinner
- **Activities:** Pompeii entry (~€13–18), boats, museums roughly **€150–250**
- **Total:** **€1440–2330 per person**

This route works well for travelers who'd rather not drive: three distinct regions of Italy (central, south, coast) with minimal navigation stress, since the train system generally runs reliably even with occasional delays.

## Route 2: Florence and Tuscany — Car from Day Four

**Days 1–3: Rome.** Same setup as above — three nights, Testaccio or Trastevere, with jet lag factored into the early schedule.

**Day 4: Rome to Florence by train.** About 1 hour 30 minutes, followed by picking up a rental car for the next 10 days.

**Days 4–14: Tuscany.** Three nights in Florence, then a Tuscan town as the car hub — Montepulciano or Montalcino both work well as real towns with year-round residents rather than short-term rental clusters.

From Florence, it's roughly 2–2.5 hours to either town. From there, day-trip range covers:
- **Pienza** (30 minutes) — a Renaissance town, good for lunch
- **Crete Senesi** (1 hour) — the rolling clay hills common in photos of the region
- **Montepulciano to Pienza** loop — doable as one longer drive
- **Chianti villages** — west of Montalcino, with wineries and cypress-lined roads

A common structure that avoids excess driving: 4 nights in Florence, then 7 nights based in a single Tuscan town (Montalcino or Montepulciano) rather than changing base every two nights — the added driving typically isn't worth the marginal extra ground covered.

**Important:** ZTL (limited-traffic zones) exist in Florence, Siena, and most Tuscan town centers. Camera-detected violations typically generate a fine in the range of roughly €80–130, arriving weeks or months later via the rental company, which usually adds its own administrative fee on top. Parking outside the centro storico and walking in avoids this entirely.

### This route costs (per person):
- **Accommodation:** €60–80/night in Rome/Florence, €50–70/night in smaller towns × 14 nights = **€770–1120**
- **Train:** Rome–Florence = **€20–70**
- **Car rental:** 10 days, mid-size automatic, roughly €40–55/day = **€400–550**
- **Gas:** roughly €40–60 for 10 days of local driving
- **Parking:** €15–25 total across towns
- **Food & coffee:** roughly **€450–650**, including one winery lunch
- **Activities:** Galleries, wine tastings, roughly **€200–350**
- **Total:** **€1890–2780 per person**

Expect an average of 1.5–2 hours of driving per day with several hours stationary — a pacing that tends to stick better in memory than covering more ground quickly.

## Route 3: Puglia — Slowest, Cheapest, Least Crowded

**Days 1–4: Rome to Bari by train, then south.** Rome to Bari on Frecciarossa runs about 4 hours, roughly €30–80 depending on booking timing — a direct route south with no connection needed.

**Days 4–14: Bari → Lecce → coast, car required.**

Puglia doesn't have a rail network dense enough for this kind of itinerary, so a car is close to essential here. Renting for 10 days in Bari (roughly €40–50/day, with the same ZTL rules as Tuscany applying in town centers) is the practical approach.

A reasonable structure: 3 nights in Lecce (the Baroque town, about 1.5 hours from Bari), then 4 nights on the Adriatic coast in Otranto or Polignano a Mare, then 3 nights back near Bari to catch a departing flight.

From Lecce, day trips include:
- **Otranto** (45 minutes) — cliff town, castle, pebble beach
- **Gallipoli** (1 hour) — lagoon-side fishing village
- **Santa Maria di Leuca** (1.5 hours) — the southern tip, largely for the scenery

This region genuinely sees fewer tourists than the north, particularly in peak summer months when crowds concentrate around Venice and Tuscany.

### This route costs (per person):
- **Accommodation:** €40–65/night (Puglia runs cheaper) × 14 nights = **€560–910**
- **Train:** Rome–Bari = **€35–70**
- **Car rental:** 10 days × €40–50 = **€400–500**
- **Gas:** €30–50
- **Parking:** roughly €20 total (notably cheaper than Tuscany)
- **Food:** orecchiette, fresh seafood, roughly €300–450 for generally good value
- **Activities:** Minimal entry fees, mostly coastal walking, **€80–150**
- **Total:** **€1505–2180 per person**

This is generally the budget-friendliest of the three routes: fewer crowds, lower prices, and a distinctly different side of Italy — the tradeoff being more time spent driving.

## The One-Move Rule: Change Base No More Than Three Times

Every time luggage gets packed and a place changed in Italy, there's roughly 90 minutes lost to packing, 45 minutes to travel, and another hour getting oriented in the new place — nearly three hours of a day gone to logistics.

Two weeks means 14 days. Moving base every two nights means moving seven times — potentially 21 hours of lost time to transit and settling in.

A workable rule: **change base no more than three times.** That typically looks like:
- **Rome** (nights 1–5)
- **Tuscany or coast** (nights 6–11)
- **One final place** (nights 12–14), or staying put in the night-6 location through the end

Fewer moves means more breathing room in each day, a better chance of finding a favorite coffee spot by day three, and no suitcase hauled up multiple flights of stairs more than necessary.

All three routes above follow this rule.

## What to Book Ahead, What to Leave Open

**Book these:**
- **Accommodation** — 6–8 weeks out. July and August commonly run €50+ more per night. April, May, September, and October tend to be sweet spots.
- **Trains** — 2–4 weeks ahead for Frecciarossa can save €20–40 per ticket. Regional trains (Naples–Salerno) typically don't need advance booking, running roughly every 30 minutes.
- **Rental car** — 2–3 weeks out. Collision damage waivers and coverage details are worth reading carefully before signing.

**Leave open:**
- **Museums** — Most aren't consistently full except in peak August; walking up and paying at the door is often workable outside the busiest days, though popular sights (Uffizi, Vatican) are the exception and are worth pre-booking.
- **Restaurant reservations** — Italians generally eat late (9–10 PM). Dinner at 7 PM often means many tables are still open.
- **Day trips** — Flexible plans handle bad weather or a change of interest better than a locked schedule.

## The Bottom Line

Two weeks isn't enough to "really know Italy" — but it is enough to know one region well and come home with real memories rather than a photo album shot at a run.

For a first trip, picking one route and sticking with it tends to work better than trying to cover the north and south in one go. Travelers attempting Rome–Florence–Venice–Amalfi in 14 days often look worn out by day eight, having seen many things briefly instead of a few things properly.

A useful benchmark: a strong Italy trip often involves three or more days in one town, long enough to start recognizing the regulars at a favorite coffee bar. That kind of familiarity rarely survives an itinerary built to look impressive on paper.

August tends to be hot, expensive, and the month many Italian businesses close for their own holidays — worth avoiding where the schedule allows. September is generally a better bet, and spring (May, early June) is often considered close to ideal.

Between the three routes, Route 1 or Route 3 tend to suit most first-time visitors better than Route 2, since Tuscany's charms are widely covered elsewhere — spending one week south of Rome and a second settling into a single town, moving at a slower and more local pace, tends to be the version of the trip most travelers remember fondly.
