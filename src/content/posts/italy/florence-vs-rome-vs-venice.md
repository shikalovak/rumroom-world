A week in Italy and three names that mean different things to everyone who's been: Rome sounds mandatory, Florence feels essential, and Venice is on every poster while also getting a reputation for being overcrowded and overrated by 2pm. So which one actually deserves the days?

These three cities are nothing alike, which is exactly why comparing them matters. The real question isn't which city is "best" — it's which city matches the kind of trip being planned.

## The Question Behind the Question: What Kind of Days Are Wanted?

Before comparing prices or walking times, it's worth asking: are these days meant for seeing things, or for being somewhere?

Rome is about seeing things — dozens of them. The Colosseum. The Vatican. The Roman Forum. Dozens more spread across a city so vast that a good chunk of any day goes to the metro or resting sore feet.

Florence is about density. Nearly everything sits within walking distance of everything else. The Duomo, Uffizi Gallery, Palazzo Vecchio, Ponte Vecchio are close enough to loop back to twice before lunch.

Venice is about the atmosphere of being in Venice. There are museums and a basilica, sure, but the city itself is the main attraction — the canals, the bridges, the total absence of cars. Approaching it with a checklist mindset tends to undercut the whole point.

A useful reframe: instead of "which city is best," ask "how many box-ticking moments do I actually want?" The answer points toward the right city.

## Rome — Scale, Layers, and the Exhaustion That Comes With It

Rome tends to exhaust visitors — not as a criticism, just a fact of its size. Expect a lot of walking, a lot to see, and sore feet by evening.

Rome's historic centre is roughly 10 times larger than Florence's. Crossing it end-to-end isn't a 30-minute walk; it calls for a metro ride. The city never quite feels "covered" — there's always another neighbourhood, another church, another ruin.

A common pattern for a four-day trip: the Colosseum around 11am after a metro ride, then the attached Roman Forum, then a museum across the piazza, lunch by 1:30pm, then the Pantheon after another metro ride or a 20-minute walk. By 4pm, five major sites are behind you and energy is fully spent.

Rome tends to reward a longer stay, since depth matters more than speed here. Even two weeks might not feel like "finishing" it — which is arguably part of the appeal. The city is simply too big to "do" in four days; a longer list of things missed is the natural result.

### Accommodation and Neighborhoods

Budget rooms in Rome run roughly **€50–80 a night** for a very basic place, often a metro ride from anything central. Mid-range hotels (3–4 stars, clean, decent location) run roughly **€90–150** per night. The most convenient neighbourhoods — Centro Storico, Trastevere, Testaccio — cost accordingly.

On [Booking.com](https://www.booking.com/searchresults.html?ss=Italy), the Navona/Pantheon area is noisy but central, Trastevere is charming with steep stairs and further from the metro, and Testaccio is quieter, more residential, and generally more affordable — a reasonable starting point for 3-star options around €80–110 that still connect well by bus.

### Getting Around

The metro is the most efficient option, and a 7-day pass runs roughly **€33**. Taxis are comparatively expensive and worth avoiding for routine trips. Buses are cheap but take some getting used to.

### Costs: Daily Budget

A meal at a non-tourist restaurant: roughly **€12–16**. A pizza slice: **€3–4**. A coffee at the bar: **€1.50–2**. Colosseum entry: around **€18** (booking ahead is strongly worth it — walk-up queues can eat 90 minutes). Vatican Museums: **€20** at the door, with online booking typically adding a booking fee (often bringing the total to around €25) — the trade-off is skipping the walk-up line.

Daily estimate (excluding accommodation): roughly €60–80 for simple meals plus paid sites.

## Florence — Walkable, Dense, Runs Out After a Few Days

Florence is the opposite of Rome: everything is close, which means it's possible to see a great deal very quickly — sometimes too quickly.

The historic centre is walkable end-to-end in about 30–40 minutes. The Duomo, Uffizi, Palazzo Vecchio, Accademia (Michelangelo's David), and Ponte Vecchio all sit within a 15-minute radius, making it realistic to visit several major sights before lunch.

This is both Florence's strength and its limitation. By day three, the major sights are typically covered. By day four, a repeat visit to somewhere already seen often starts to feel familiar rather than fresh.

### Why People Love It (and Why They Leave)

Florence rewards slowing down, but a shorter visit rarely allows for that. A food tour, a gelato crawl, wandering the smaller churches, sitting in a piazza with a book — these make up the deeper Florence experience, but they take a level of unhurried time that a quick visit doesn't allow.

Checking the main boxes takes about two days. Actually settling into the city's slower rhythm takes at least five. There's not much of a comfortable middle ground.

### Accommodation

Budget options run roughly **€40–70** for basic rooms, often a 15-minute walk from the centre. Mid-range runs **€80–130** for a real 3-star in the centre. Oltrarno, south of Ponte Vecchio, tends to offer the best value — quieter, typically €15–20 cheaper per night, and less crowded than the area near the Duomo.

### Costs: Daily Budget

A meal: roughly **€14–18** at a casual spot. Pizza: **€4–5**. Coffee: **€1–2**. Uffizi Gallery: around **€18** (booking online recommended). Duomo climb: around **€8** (sometimes included in combo tickets). Accademia: around **€12**. A realistic daily estimate is **€50–70**, excluding accommodation, for museums plus regular meals.

## Venice — Extraordinary Early and Late, Crowded at Noon

Venice's experience depends heavily on timing. At 9am, the light is soft, the water is still, and the narrow alleys are genuinely walkable. By 12:30pm, the same streets can feel like a crowded thoroughfare. By 7pm, once most day-trippers have left, the city settles back into something closer to a real place. Arriving mid-afternoon and judging the whole city from that window can give a skewed impression.

### The Reality of Venice

Venice is genuinely tiny — walkable end to end in about 25–30 minutes. It's also expensive, largely because of the economics of a city with no room to expand that hosts several million visitors a year.

Venice tends to make more sense as an overnight stop or a slice of a longer Italy trip than as a standalone multi-day destination. One night captures both the evening calm and the early-morning light. Two nights allow for a genuinely slower pace. Beyond that, many travelers find themselves inventing reasons to stay rather than needing the extra time.

### The Day-Tripper Fee (and Why It Matters)

Venice has run a day-tripper access fee (the *contributo di accesso*) on a set of dates each year since 2024, applying only on specific, mostly spring/early-summer weekend and holiday dates (roughly 60 dates across April–July in 2026), from 8:30 AM to 4 PM. The fee runs **€5 if booked at least four days ahead, or €10 for last-minute bookings**. Overnight visitors are generally exempt, but the exemption isn't automatic — it typically requires registering through the official Venezia Unica system (often with help from the accommodation) to get an exemption voucher. Since these dates and rules have changed year to year, checking the current official schedule before a trip is worth the few minutes it takes, rather than assuming last year's dates apply.

### Accommodation

This is where Venice gets expensive. Budget rooms start around **€70–90** per night for something basic. Mid-range runs **€120–180**. Genuinely cheap options are rare, given the logistics of building and maintaining anything on the lagoon. Comparing prices with the nearby mainland town of Mestre can save roughly **€30–40 per night**, at the cost of losing the canal views — a trade-off that may make more sense for a two-night stay than a single night, where staying in Venice itself usually wins.

### Costs: Daily Budget

A meal away from St. Mark's Square: roughly **€16–22**. Pizza to-go: **€5–6**. Coffee: **€2–2.50**. Basilica di San Marco: around **€5** for the interior (looking from outside is free). Additional sights, like the Doge's Palace, run around €20. A realistic daily budget, excluding accommodation, is roughly **€60–80**.

### Getting There

Florence to Venice by train takes roughly **2 hours 5 minutes**. The Frecciarossa (fast train) runs roughly **€35–60 if booked ahead**, rising to **€70–100** at walk-up prices — booking online several days in advance is generally worth it.

## Crowds by Month, City by City

Seasonal timing changes each of these cities considerably.

**Rome:** Peak crowds June–September, with Easter particularly intense. October–November tends to be close to ideal — warm enough, without oppressive heat, and noticeably less crowded. January–March is cold but walkable and quiet.

**Florence:** A similar pattern — peak June–August, better in September, lovely April–May, and October often considered ideal. December has its own charm, though holiday season draws its own crowds.

**Venice:** Even more seasonal than the others. Summer tends to be uncomfortably hot and crowded. September starts cooling down with fewer visitors. October is frequently cited as the best month — mild weather, fewer tourists, good light. November–February is cold and quiet. April–May sees a rebound in spring tourism.

For flexible dates, April, September, or October tend to work well across all three cities. Locked into summer travel generally means expecting crowds everywhere and planning to move around rather than anchor in one place.

## Cost Comparison: The Full Picture

An approximate breakdown for a week in each city (accommodation, food, transport, major sights):

**Rome:**
- Accommodation (mid-range, 7 nights): €630–1050
- Food & coffee daily: €60–80 × 7 = €420–560
- Major sights (Colosseum, Vatican, etc.): €100–140
- Local transport (7-day pass): €33
- **Total: roughly €1183–1783**

**Florence:**
- Accommodation (mid-range, 7 nights): €560–910
- Food & coffee daily: €50–70 × 7 = €350–490
- Major sights (Uffizi, Duomo, Accademia): €50–80
- Local transport: €25 (buses, minimal metro)
- **Total: roughly €985–1475**

**Venice:**
- Accommodation (mid-range, 1–2 nights): €120–360
- Food & coffee daily: €60–80 × 1–2 days = €60–160
- Sights (Basilica, maybe one gallery): €25–40
- Vaporetto water-bus pass (48 hours): €25
- Day-tripper fee (if applicable): €5–10
- **Total for 1–2 nights: roughly €235–595**

For a week-long trip, Venice tends to work best as *part* of the itinerary rather than the anchor — Florence or Rome as the base, with Venice as an overnight (about a 2-hour train ride from Florence).

## How Long Each City Actually Holds

**Rome** comfortably holds attention for 5–7 days for anyone interested in history and reasonably tolerant of walking. Ten days allows time to reach into neighborhoods and smaller sites. Fourteen days genuinely exhausts most of what's there.

**Florence** works well for 3–4 days at an efficient pace, or 5–6 days at a slower one with cafés and smaller galleries built in. Beyond six days, most visitors are looking for reasons to stay rather than things to do.

**Venice** works best over one to two days — one night as a minimum, two as a comfortable maximum. Beyond that, the value proposition starts to decline given the cost.

For a seven-day trip, a reasonable split is three days each in Rome and Florence, with one day in Venice. For ten days: four days each in Rome and Florence, two days (one night) in Venice. For five days, see the next section.

## If There Are Only Five Days, Pick This One

Rome tends to be the strongest choice for a short stay.

Florence's Renaissance art and Venice's canals will be missed, but Rome is the only one of the three that genuinely rewards a short visit. The highlights — Colosseum, Roman Forum, Pantheon, Vatican, Trevi Fountain — fit into four days without feeling completely rushed, and Rome's core appeal (layers of history stacked in one place) comes through even on a compressed schedule.

Florence is arguably the more refined experience of the two, but it needs more time to click. Venice works best as an add-on rather than the centerpiece of a short trip.

For anyone still leaning toward Florence or Venice for five days: Florence works reasonably well as a five-day solo trip (roughly three days on art, two on slower wandering), though the time crunch will be noticeable. Venice for five days alone is a harder sell — either the cost of staying that long starts to feel disproportionate, or the trip gets cut short and feels rushed either way.

### The Train Option

Using Rome as a base for day trips: Rome to Florence runs about **1h30 on the Frecciarossa**, roughly **€20–50 booked ahead**. From Florence, a Frecciarossa to Venice runs about **2h05**, roughly **€25–60**. It's doable but tiring as a single-day round trip — generally, picking one city to actually stay in works better than day-tripping between all three.

## The Bottom Line

Here's the less popular take: Venice might not be the right fit for every trip, and that's a legitimate conclusion, not a failure to appreciate it.

Venice earns its reputation, but mostly in specific windows of the day, and mostly for travelers not treating it as a box to check. On a tight timeline and tight budget, skipping Venice in favor of Rome plus Florence is often the more practical choice — more seen, less spent on overpriced food near the canals.

Rome overwhelms by design — there's more history stacked in one place than any single trip can absorb, which is part of what makes it compelling rather than a flaw. Florence fits into a manageable mental map by day three, which is its own kind of success.

Venice is the one that asks for patience. Given that, it rewards it; without it, it tends to feel expensive and crowded. Skipping it on a particular trip isn't a wrong answer.

These cities aren't really competing with each other — they're built around different questions. Rome asks whether you can handle beauty at scale. Florence asks whether you can see everything up close. Venice asks whether you can slow down. The right pick depends on which question fits the trip.
