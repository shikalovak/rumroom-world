You've got a week in Italy and you're staring at three names that mean different things to everyone who's been. Rome sounds mandatory. Florence feels essential. Venice is on the posters, but people also say it's overcrowded at 2pm and overrated. So which one actually deserves your days?

I've spent time in all three — multiple trips, different seasons, different moods. They're nothing alike, which is why comparing them matters. The answer isn't which city is "best." It's which city matches the kind of trip you're actually taking.

## The Question Behind the Question: What Kind of Days Do You Want?

Before I compare prices or walking times, ask yourself this first: **Are you here to see things, or to be somewhere?**

Rome is about seeing things. There are dozens of them. The Colosseum. The Vatican. The Roman Forum. Dozens more spread across a city so vast you'll spend half your time either on the metro or resting sore feet.

Florence is about density. You can hit nearly everything on foot in a morning. The Duomo, Uffizi Gallery, Palazzo Vecchio, Ponte Vecchio — they're close enough that you can loop back twice before lunch if you want.

Venice is about the atmosphere of being in Venice. There's museums and a Basilica, yes, but honestly, the city itself *is* the thing. The canals, the bridges, the fact that cars don't exist — that's what you're paying for. The second you arrive thinking "what do I need to check off here?" you've already lost it.

**My take:** If you're asking "which city is best," you're asking the wrong way. Ask instead: "How many box-ticking moments do I actually want?" That answer points you toward which city fits.

## Rome — Scale, Layers, and the Exhaustion That Comes With It

Rome exhausts you. I don't mean that negatively — I mean it literally. You will walk a lot, see a lot, and your feet will hurt.

The historic centre of Rome is roughly **10 times larger than Florence's**. End-to-end is not a 30-minute walk; it's a metro ride. This means the city never feels conquered. There's always another neighbourhood, another church, another neighbourhood ruin.

I gave myself four days in Rome once thinking I'd "do it." By the end of day three, I wasn't discovering; I was moving between checkpoints. The Colosseum at 11am comes after a 25-minute metro ride from your hotel. Then the Roman Forum (attached, no problem). Then the Palazzo dei Conservatori across the piazza. Then you need lunch (it's now 1:30pm). Then the Pantheon (another metro ride, or a 20-minute walk in heat). By 4pm, you've seen five major sites and you're spent.

**Real take:** Rome rewards a longer stay because depth matters more than speed. You could spend two weeks here and still not feel like you've *finished* it. Honestly, it's better that way. The city is too big to "do" in four days; you just accumulate a longer list of things you missed.

### Accommodation and Neighborhoods

Budget rooms in Rome run **€50–80 a night** for a very basic place, but that often means a metro ride from anything interesting. Mid-range hotels (3–4 stars, clean, decent location) are **€90–150** per night. The good neighbourhoods — Centro Storico, Trastevere, Testaccio — are where you want to be, and that costs.

With [Booking.com](https://www.booking.com/searchresults.html?ss=Italy), look for the Navona/Pantheon area (noisy but central), Trastevere (charming, steep stairs, far from metro), or Testaccio (quieter, real Romans, more affordable). I'd actually do is check for 3-star hotels in Testaccio first — you pay €80–110, it feels lived-in, and you're off the tourist treadmill but still connected by buses.

### Getting Around

The metro is your friend, and a 7-day pass is **€33**. Taxis are expensive; avoid them. Buses are cheap and confusing until they're not.

### Costs: Daily Budget

A meal at a non-tourist restaurant: **€12–16**. A pizza slice: **€3–4**. A coffee at the bar: **€1.50–2**. Colosseum entry: **€18** (book ahead, seriously). Vatican Museums: **€19** if you book, €21 walk-up. Quick tip: book everything online in advance; queues without reservation can eat 90 minutes.

Daily estimate (not including accommodation): €60–80 if you're eating simply and seeing paid sites.

## Florence — Walkable, Dense, Runs Out After Four Days

Florence is the opposite of Rome: everything is close, which means you can see everything *fast*. Too fast.

The historic centre is genuinely walkable end-to-end in about 30–40 minutes. The Duomo, Uffizi, Palazzo Vecchio, Accademia (Michelangelo's David), Ponte Vecchio — they're all within a 15-minute radius. I've had mornings where I hit four major museums before lunch.

This is Florence's strength and its limitation. By day three, you've seen the major sights. By day four, you're already revisiting them because the walk-through-the-city routine feels familiar.

**Honestly,** I booked Florence for six nights on my second Italy trip because I thought I'd "do it properly." By day five, I was bored. There's beautiful art, sure, and the city itself is stunning. But once you've climbed the Duomo, crossed Ponte Vecchio twice, and sat in the Uffizi for three hours, the main event is over.

### Why People Love It (and Why They Leave)

Florence rewards slowness, but most people don't give it the time it needs to click into slowness. A food tour, a gelato crawl, wandering the smaller churches, sitting in a piazza with a book — these are the actual Florence experiences. But they take intention.

If you're checking boxes, Florence takes two days. If you're *living* in Florence, it takes at least five. There's almost no middle ground.

### Accommodation

Budget options: **€40–70** for basic rooms, often in the surrounding areas (still 15 minutes walk). Mid-range: **€80–130** for a real 3-star in the centre. The best value is Oltrarno, south of Ponte Vecchio — quieter, cheaper by €15–20 per night, and honestly nicer than the crush near the Duomo.

### Costs: Daily Budget

A meal: **€14–18** at a casual spot. Pizza: **€4–5**. Coffee: **€1–2**. Uffizi Gallery: **€18** (book online). Duomo climb: **€8** (included in some combo tickets). Accademia: **€12**. You'll spend **€50–70 daily** excluding accommodation if you're seeing museums and eating normally.

## Venice — Extraordinary Early and Late, Unbearable at Noon

I gave Venice one night on my first trip and it was the wrong call. I arrived at 2pm, saw canals full of tour groups in matching hats, ate an overpriced pizza on a bridge, and left thinking it was a theme park. Wrong.

Venice at 9am is magic. The light is soft, the water is still, and you can actually walk through the narrow alleys without bumping shoulders. Venice at 7pm, after most day-trippers drain out, becomes a real city again. Venice at 12:30pm is a mob scene. This city's entire experience depends on *when* you're there, not just *if* you're there.

### The Reality of Venice

Venice is tiny — genuinely tiny. You can walk from one end to the other in 25–30 minutes. But it's also brutally expensive and designed entirely around extracting money from tourists. This isn't cynicism; it's just the economics of a city that has no room to expand and gets four million visitors a year.

**My take:** Venice makes sense as an overnight or a part of a longer Italy trip, not as a three-day destination. One night gives you the 7pm atmosphere and the 9am departure light. Two nights lets you actually slow down. Three nights and you're inventing reasons to stay.

### The Day-Tripper Fee (and Why It Matters)

Venice introduced a €5–10 entry fee on peak days in 2024, applicable to day-trippers registering after they arrive. If you're staying overnight, you don't pay it. If you're coming as a day trip from Florence or another city, budget an extra €5–10, and the fees change year to year, so confirm the current dates before you go.

### Accommodation

This is where Venice gets brutal. Budget rooms start at **€70–90** per night, and that's basic. Mid-range runs **€120–180**. There are no cheap deals in Venice; the lagoon logistics make everything expensive. What I'd actually do is compare prices between Venice itself and the nearby mainland town of Mestre — you save **€30–40 per night**, lose the canals, but gain €30–40. For a one-night stay, stay in Venice. For two nights, consider splitting.

### Costs: Daily Budget

A meal (not on St. Mark's Square): **€16–22**. Pizza to-go: **€5–6**. Coffee: €2–2.50. Basilica di San Marco: **€5** for entry, free to look. The galleries and museums are extras (Doge's Palace, €20). A real daily budget (excluding accommodation): **€60–80**.

### Getting There

Florence to Venice by train: approximately **2 hours 5 minutes**. The Frecciarossa (fast train) is **€35–60 if booked ahead**, €70–100 walk-up. Honestly, book it online days in advance; walk-up pricing stings.

## Crowds by Month, City by City

This matters more than you think. Rome and Venice are different cities in June vs November.

**Rome:** Peak crowds June–September. Easter is hell. October–November is honestly perfect — warm enough, no oppressive heat, manageable crowds. January–March is cold but walkable and quiet.

**Florence:** Similar pattern. Peak June–August. September is better. April–May is lovely. October is perfect. December feels beautiful but holiday decorations attract crowds too.

**Venice:** Even more seasonal. Summer is unbearable (sweltering and mobbed). September starts cooling down and crowds thin. October is possibly the best month — weather is mild, tourists drop off, and the light is golden. November–February is cold and quiet. April–May is crowded again as spring tourists arrive.

**Real take:** If you're flexible on dates, aim for April, September, or October for any of these cities. If you're locked into summer travel, expect crowds everywhere and plan to move around rather than anchor down.

## Cost Comparison: The Full Picture

Here's an honest breakdown for a week in each city (accommodation, food, transport, major sights):

**Rome:**
- Accommodation (mid-range, 7 nights): €630–1050
- Food & coffee daily: €60–80 × 7 = €420–560
- Major sights (Colosseum, Vatican, etc.): €100–140
- Local transport (7-day pass): €33
- **Total: €1183–1783**

**Florence:**
- Accommodation (mid-range, 7 nights): €560–910
- Food & coffee daily: €50–70 × 7 = €350–490
- Major sights (Uffizi, Duomo, Accademia): €50–80
- Local transport: €25 (buses, minimal metro)
- **Total: €985–1475**

**Venice:**
- Accommodation (mid-range, 1–2 nights): €120–360
- Food & coffee daily: €60–80 × 1–2 days = €60–160
- Sights (Basilica, maybe one gallery): €25–40
- Vaporetto water-bus pass (48 hours): €25
- Day-tripper fee (if applicable): €5–10
- **Total for 1–2 nights: €235–595**

If you're building a week, Venice works best as *part* of the trip, not the anchor. Florence or Rome as the base, with Venice as an overnight from Florence (2h train ride).

## How Long Each City Actually Holds

This is the honest part that nobody says directly.

**Rome** can hold you for 5–7 days easily if you're into history and don't mind walking. At 10 days, you're reaching deep into neighborhoods and smaller sites. At 14 days, you're genuinely exhausting it.

**Florence** genuinely works for 3–4 days if you're efficient, or 5–6 days if you're slowing down with cafes and smaller galleries. More than six days and you're inventing reasons to stay.

**Venice** is two days maximum, one day minimum. More than two and you're paying premium prices for diminishing returns.

**What I'd actually do is:** if you have seven days, give Rome three days, Florence three days, and Venice one day. If you have ten days, Rome four days, Florence four days, Venice two days (one night). If you have five days, see the next section.

## If You Only Have Five Days, Pick This One

This is the question, really.

Rome. Pick Rome.

Yes, you'll miss Florence's Renaissance and Venice's canals. But Rome is the *only* city of the three that genuinely rewards a short stay. You can do the highlights — Colosseum, Roman Forum, Pantheon, Vatican, Trevi Fountain — in four days without feeling completely rushed. You'll see the thing that people came to Rome to see: layers of history in one place.

Florence is better than Rome for what it is, but it needs time you don't have. Venice is best as an add-on, not a priority.

If you *absolutely* want Florence or Venice instead: Florence works okay as a five-day solo trip (three days seeing art, two days being human), but you'll feel the time crunch. Venice doesn't work for five days alone — you'll either leave frustrated because you're paying premium prices for a short stay, or you'll extend and regret the money spent.

### The Train Option

If you're picking Rome as your base and want to day-trip: Rome to Florence is a **1h30 Frecciarossa**, **€20–50 booked ahead**. From Florence, you could Frecciarossa to Venice (**2h05, €25–60**). It's doable but tiring. You're better off picking one and staying.

## My Honest Take

Here's what I'll tell you that costs me credibility: **Venice might not be for you, and that's okay.**

I see the postcards. I know it's supposed to be magical. And it is — but only in very specific windows of the day and only if you're not treating it like a box to check. If you're on a tight timeline and a tight budget, skipping Venice and doing Rome plus Florence is the more honest choice. You'll see more, enjoy more, and spend less money on overpriced pasta.

Rome overwhelms people, but that overwhelm is part of the experience. There's so much history stacked on itself that you can't see it all, and that's actually freeing. Florence fits inside your head by day three, which is also fine — it means you've *gotten* it.

Venice is the one that asks you to slow down. If you can't, it'll just feel expensive and crowded. And you know what? That's a legitimate reason not to go on *this* trip.

The cities aren't in competition with each other. They're asking different questions. Rome asks, "Can you handle beauty at scale?" Florence asks, "Can you see everything in close focus?" Venice asks, "Can you stop?" Pick the question that matches where you're at.
