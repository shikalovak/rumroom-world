France has no dedicated "digital nomad visa." What actually exists is the long-stay **visiteur** visa (VLS-TS), designed for people with independent income who plan to live in France without working for a French employer. Remote work for non-French clients sits in a legal grey area, but it's the route most digital nomads use. This guide walks through how it works, what you'll need, and what happens once you land — verify every detail against [France-Visas](https://france-visas.gouv.fr/) and your specific consulate before applying, since requirements and fees are updated periodically.

## The Visiteur Visa: What Nomads Actually Use

The **VLS-TS visiteur** is a one-year renewable residence permit for people with stable income outside France. It isn't marketed as "for nomads," but nomads use it because it's the long-stay option that doesn't require a job offer, student enrollment, or investor status.

Weighing the alternatives — talent passport, entrepreneur visa, or trying to stretch a tourist visa — this is usually the only realistic path for someone without specific professional credentials or an employer sponsor.

As of 2026, the consular visa application fee is roughly **€99** (exact fees vary by consulate — verify with yours). That covers the visa itself. There's a separate step after you arrive: validating with OFII, which now carries its own cost (see below) and is where most people get tripped up.

## Visiteur vs. Other Routes: Why This One?

**Tourist visa (Schengen):** Gives you 90 days in any 180-day window. Not renewable from inside France, and there's no reliable legal way to extend it through repeated border crossings ("border runs") — immigration authorities treat that as visa abuse.

**Talent passport:** Requires a qualifying degree, specific professional criteria, or high recognized income depending on the category. Most remote workers without a sponsoring employer or specific credentials won't qualify.

**Entrepreneur visa:** Needs a business bank account, a French business plan, and French business registration. Workable if you're formally setting up a business in France, but more expensive and time-consuming than the visitor route.

**Long-stay visitor (VLS-TS visiteur):** No job required. Income needs to be stable and sufficient to live on (tied to the French minimum wage — see below) or covered by equivalent savings. This is the route most nomads actually qualify for.

## Who Qualifies: The Income & Savings Bar

France's "visiteur" visa is designed for **people with independent income** — retirees, remote workers, freelancers. Consulates want proof you can support yourself without needing French public assistance.

**What you need to show:**
- Stable monthly income at least equal to the net French minimum wage (SMIC) — as of mid-2026 this floor is roughly **€1,478/month net** (about €17,735/year), though the exact figure is revised periodically and can change again before you apply. Many consulates in practice look for meaningfully more than the bare legal minimum — some guidance points to 1.5–2× the SMIC (roughly €2,200–€3,000/month) as a safer target, though this varies by consulate and isn't a published universal rule.
- OR savings/investments that comfortably cover a year's living costs — figures cited by applicants and consulates commonly range from roughly €15,000–€20,000+, but this is consulate-dependent and not a fixed published threshold
- OR a combination of both

**What actually matters for documentation:** how clearly your funds are presented. If you're showing digital nomad income — Stripe payouts, freelance platform deposits, overseas client payments — bank statements going back 6–12 months are standard. A [Wise](https://wise.com/invite/irhc/kseniias372) multi-currency account, showing consistent USD or EUR deposits with clear transfers between accounts, tends to be easier for a consular officer to read than statements from several different international platforms.

Because the exact income/savings bar varies by consulate and changes with the SMIC, confirm the current figure and your specific consulate's practice directly before applying — don't rely on any single blog's numbers, including this one.

## Documents Checklist: What Actually Trips People Up

**Required documents (verify with YOUR consulate — France-Visas is the official portal):**

- **Passport** (valid for several months beyond your visa's planned end date — check your consulate's specific requirement, as this varies)
- **Completed visa application form** (Cerfa form, download from your consulate's website)
- **Notarized or certified passport copy** (a plain photocopy is often not accepted — check your consulate's requirement)
- **Proof of income** (6–12 months of bank statements, contracts, tax returns, depending on your income type)
- **Proof of accommodation** in France (lease, property deed, or a formal hosting letter — an accommodation address is generally required at application, not something to sort out after arrival)
- **Proof of health insurance** (required; more on this below)
- **Blank visa pages** in your passport (most consulates want at least a couple of blank pages)

Common issues applicants report:
- Submitting a plain, unnotarized passport copy instead of a certified one
- Submitting bank statements in a language other than French or English without translation
- Missing or incomplete accommodation proof
- Passport validity that doesn't meet the consulate's specific minimum

## Health Insurance: The Non-Negotiable Requirement

You cannot get a VLS-TS visiteur visa without proof of health insurance. Consulates are strict about this and will reject an application without adequate coverage.

What generally counts:
- **Private travel/expat health insurance** (typically the most reliable option)
- **Home-country travel insurance** (only if it explicitly covers France for the full visa duration)
- **International nomad insurance**, provided it meets the coverage minimum your specific consulate requires

**Practical step:** Email your consulate's visa office directly and ask what health insurance minimum they require for the VLS-TS visiteur visa — requirements aren't fully standardized across consulates.

## After You Land: The OFII Validation Step

Getting approved for the visa is step one. Validating it after you arrive is step two, and it has to happen within **3 months of arrival**.

**What happens:**

1. You arrive in France with your VLS-TS visa in your passport.
2. You complete validation online through the government portal (administration-etrangers-en-france.interieur.gouv.fr), providing your visa details, entry date, and French address.
3. **There is a validation fee.** As of 2026, this is a paid step — reporting on current rates puts the standard validation fee at roughly **€300** (with a reduced rate of around €100 for certain categories, such as some students). This is a change worth budgeting for: it is not free, contrary to older or informal guides that describe it that way. Confirm the exact current amount on the official portal before you pay, as fees are adjusted periodically.
4. OFII may follow up for additional steps, which for the visiteur category can include a medical appointment depending on your situation — check what applies to you on the official portal.
5. Once validated, keep your confirmation/receipt until you receive any further residence documentation.

Once validated, you have legal residence status for the duration of your visa. You can work remotely for non-French clients, open a French bank account, and rent an apartment under that status.

## Timeline & Costs: What to Actually Budget

**Application to approval:** typically several weeks to a few months, varying significantly by consulate.

**Consular visa fee:** roughly €99 (verify with your consulate).

**OFII validation fee:** roughly €300 standard rate as of 2026 (reduced rate for some categories) — confirm the current amount on the official portal, as this has increased in recent years and can change again.

**Total out-of-pocket for the visa and validation:** roughly €400+ once you include both the consular fee and OFII validation, plus document prep (notarization, translation), which can add another €50–150 depending on your country.

**Total cost to you as a nomad:** factor in flights to your consulate if you live abroad, accommodation while waiting, and your first month's rent in France. Realistic total cost is often €1,000–3,000, most of which is living expenses rather than visa fees.

**A general planning timeline (actual pace varies a lot by consulate):**
- Early: gather documents, get your passport copy notarized/certified
- Next: submit your application
- Following weeks to months: wait for approval, following up with your consulate as needed
- After approval: travel to France
- Within 3 months of arrival: validate with OFII

## The Bottom Line

France's long-stay visa isn't designed with digital nomads specifically in mind — it's a visitor visa built for retirees and people with independent income. Remote work for non-French clients is a grey area: the law says you can't work for a French employer on this visa, but it doesn't explicitly forbid freelancing for international clients. Most people interpret this as workable; some consulates may take a stricter view. There's a degree of legal ambiguity here that's worth understanding before you rely on this route.

For most remote workers who want a legal, stable way to stay in France for a year, this visa is the most realistic option compared to the alternative of repeated tourist-visa stays or border runs, which carries more risk. If you do go this route, it's worth understanding your tax obligations if you spend enough time in France to become tax resident, and getting professional advice if your situation is complicated.
