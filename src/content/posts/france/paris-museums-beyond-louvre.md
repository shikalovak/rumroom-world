The Louvre is overrated, and I'm saying that as someone who's been twice. Two hours waiting in line to see the Mona Lisa from behind a crowd of 200 people holding iPads? Not worth it. The best museums in Paris are actually **Musée d'Orsay** (Impressionist masterpieces, manageable crowds, under 2 hours well-spent), **L'Orangerie** (Monet's water lilies in a gallery built for them), **Rodin** (the sculpture garden alone justifies the visit), and **Carnavalet** (free, underrated, genuinely fascinating). Each one costs €12–16 and gives you the Paris-museum experience without the Louvre's exhausting queue theatre.

I've spent a combined three weeks hunting through Paris galleries, timing queues, and doing the math on the museum pass. Here's what actually works—and what I'd honestly skip.

## Why I stopped recommending the Louvre first

I stood in a 45-minute line at the Louvre on a Tuesday morning in May. *Tuesday*. And that was after I'd bought tickets online. The irony? Once inside, I saw more tourists than paintings. The Louvre's fame works against it: the building is genuinely impressive, but you're paying around €17 to be herded through the hallways in a sea of other people doing the same thing.

**My take:** The Louvre makes sense if you're an art historian with specific pieces to hunt. For everyone else—grab the highlights in 60 minutes and save your energy for the museums that won't destroy your patience. The Mona Lisa is small, behind bulletproof glass, and barely visible anyway.

## Musée d'Orsay: the one I'd send you to

This is the museum I recommend to absolutely everyone. It's housed in a converted railway station (Gare d'Orsay) in the 7th arrondissement, and the renovation is stunning—all that ornate 19th-century architecture now filled with Monet, Renoir, Van Gogh, and Cézanne.

**Cost:** Around €16 for a full ticket (€13 reduced).  
**Time needed:** 90 minutes if you actually want to absorb it; 45 minutes if you're rushing.  
**Crowds:** Significantly better than the Louvre. I arrived around 10:30 AM on a Wednesday and walked straight in.

The top floor is the real draw—that's where the Impressionists live. The light streaming through those massive clock windows while you're looking at *Starry Night* or Monet's haystacks creates this almost accidental perfection. Honestly, I've spent more time here than anywhere else in Paris.

Quick tip: Go early (9–10 AM) or late (after 4 PM) to miss school groups. The museum doesn't usually sell out unless you visit in July/August.

## L'Orangerie: 45 minutes, huge payoff

L'Orangerie is tucked into the 1st arrondissement near the Tuileries Gardens, and it's *intimate*. The whole point of this museum is Monet's Water Lilies series—eight massive canvases wrapped around an oval room specifically built for them. The guy who designed it understood that this wasn't a painting you look at; it's a painting you sit in.

**Cost:** Around €12.50.  
**Time needed:** 45 minutes (seriously, that's enough).  
**Crowds:** Almost never packed. I've been twice and never waited more than 5 minutes.

The ground floor has Renoir, Cézanne, Matisse—all solid work, but the water lilies upstairs are why you came. Bring a notebook. People sit on the benches and just *look* for 20 minutes straight. It's the opposite of the Louvre energy.

Real take: If you're museum-tired after Orsay, skip the ground floor and just do the water lilies. Your brain will thank you.

## Rodin: the garden is the point

The Musée Rodin is in the 7th arrondissement, and honest confession: I went for the garden and ended up spending more time there than in the museum building itself.

**Cost:** €14 full ticket (€11 reduced), or €5 if you just want the garden (and I genuinely recommend the garden-only option).  
**Time needed:** 90 minutes if you're doing both; 45 minutes if you're just garden.  
**Crowds:** Very light, even in peak season.

The sculpture garden is this hidden green space filled with Rodin's most famous works—*The Thinker*, *The Gates of Hell*, *The Kiss*—positioned among trees and hedges. It's peaceful in a way that museums almost never are. The building itself has his smaller pieces and sketches; it's well-curated, but the garden is the real experience.

If I were planning this from scratch, I'd pay €5 for the garden, grab a bench, and stay for an hour. More valuable than half the "major" museums I've been to.

## Pompidou: love it or leave it

The Centre Pompidou (Beaubourg) is that inside-out building with all the pipes and escalators on the outside in the 4th arrondissement. It's famous for being weird-looking, and the interior is modern art—photography, installation, video, experimental stuff.

**Important note:** As of 2026, the main building is in the middle of a multi-year renovation and much of the collection is not on view. Check the website before planning your visit—this may be a total miss depending on when you're going.

**Cost (when open):** Around €18.  
**Time needed:** 60–90 minutes.  
**Crowds:** Depends on what's on temporary exhibit.

**My take:** The building itself is more interesting than most of what's inside. If the renovation is still ongoing during your trip, you're not missing out. Come back when it reopens.

## Carnavalet: free, and nobody goes

Musée Carnavalet is in the 3rd arrondissement (Marais neighborhood), and it's the museum about Paris itself—the history, the streets, the old storefronts. The permanent collection is **completely free**.

**Cost:** €0 (permanent); temporary exhibits sometimes have a small fee.  
**Time needed:** 60–90 minutes.  
**Crowds:** Almost nobody. Genuinely one of the most underrated places in the city.

The rooms are organized by period—you walk through medieval Paris, Renaissance Paris, 18th-century Paris. There are old shop signs, furniture, photographs of the city as it was. It's the kind of museum that makes you understand how a place changes over time. I found myself lingering on streets I'd walked past dozens of times, suddenly seeing the history.

Quick tip: The café in the courtyard is legitimately good for a quiet break.

## Picasso: worth it if you time it

The Musée Picasso is also in the 3rd (Marais), in the Hôtel Salé, a stunning mansion. It's dedicated entirely to Picasso's work across different periods—it's the largest collection of his pieces anywhere.

**Cost:** Around €16.  
**Time needed:** 90 minutes minimum; 2 hours if you're truly interested.  
**Crowds:** Medium-heavy, especially mid-day. Go early or late.

Honestly, whether this is worth it depends on how into Picasso you are. The museum is beautiful, the work is comprehensive, but if Cubism doesn't grab you, don't force it. I found myself more engaged with the early Blue and Rose periods and less with the fractured faces of his later work, but that's a personal taste thing.

Real take: Skip if you're not particularly interested in Picasso; the Orsay collection hits more for most people.

## The 2 I'd skip

**The Louvre:** I've been clear. The fame doesn't match the experience. If you absolutely must go, buy tickets in advance, arrive by 9 AM, and give yourself 60 minutes maximum. Don't get sucked into trying to "do" the whole building. It's enormous and designed to exhaust you.

**Musée de l'Armée (Army Museum):** This one gets recommended a lot, but honestly? Unless you're genuinely interested in military history, it's a slog. Most rooms are corridor after corridor of weapons and uniforms with minimal context. I did a full lap out of obligation and regretted it. The only part worth seeing is Napoleon's Tomb, which you can see from outside for free.

## Is the Paris Museum Pass worth it? My actual math

The [Paris Museum Pass](https://parismuseumpass.com) costs roughly €70 for 2 days or €90 for 4 days, and it covers about 60 museums. On the surface, it sounds great.

Here's the math for what I actually recommend:

- Orsay: €16  
- L'Orangerie: €12.50  
- Rodin: €14  
- Picasso: €16  
- Carnavalet: €0 (free)  

**Total: €58.50 for a solid museum day** (5–6 hours of actual time). Add a couple of smaller museums and you might hit €70, but you're also spending 10+ hours in museums, which honestly feels like work.

My take: Skip the pass unless you're planning to spend 4 full days in galleries. A 2-day pass would save you maybe €15–20 and require you to sprint between places to make it worthwhile. Not worth the stress.

## Queue timing that works

I've timed this obsessively. Here's what actually works:

**9–10 AM:** All the morning-person tourists. Expect 10–15 minute lines at major museums. The best window is 10–11 AM—people have already arrived, but the lunch crowds haven't started.

**12–2 PM:** Lunch break for locals, but peak for group tours. Avoid entirely.

**4–6 PM:** The sweet spot. Most tourists are eating or museum-fatigued. You'll have plenty of breathing room. This is when I try to hit my second museum of the day.

If you're staying central (Marais, Latin Quarter, or [Booking.com](https://www.booking.com/searchresults.html?ss=Paris) has solid options in both neighborhoods), you can hit one museum early, have a proper lunch, and do another in the late afternoon without feeling rushed.

Also: Check if museums have separate tickets for different areas. Orsay's clock room sometimes has its own entry during peak times—ask at the ticket counter if there's a shorter queue.

## My honest take

Here's the thing I don't see written a lot: Paris museums are amazing *precisely because* you don't need to cram them all in. You don't need the Louvre. You don't need the Picasso if abstract work doesn't move you. You definitely don't need the Army Museum.

The best Paris museums (Orsay, L'Orangerie, Rodin's garden) are designed for breathing. They assume you might look at something for five minutes and feel fine about it. The Louvre is designed like a checklist—*Did you see the Mona Lisa? Did you see Winged Victory? Check, check, check.* It treats art like stamps to collect.

My actual advice: Pick three museums, spend a full 90 minutes in each, have a long lunch between them, and walk the neighborhoods in between. Skip the museum pass. The queues will be manageable. You'll actually remember what you saw. And you'll have time to stumble into a café or browse a used bookstall, which is honestly why you came to Paris.
