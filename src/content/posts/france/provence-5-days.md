Five days in Provence is short, but it's enough to hit the classic highlights: lavender fields that look like Impressionist paintings, medieval villages stacked onto hillsides, farmers' markets packed with produce, and wine that tastes exactly how it should in the afternoon sun. The key is basing yourself in Aix-en-Provence and being ruthless about not trying to cram 10 days into 5. This itinerary skips the overcrowded Orange amphitheater and beach-town traffic, and instead focuses on what makes Provence actually feel like Provence.

## TL;DR: The 5-Day Shape & Best Months

**The route:** Aix-en-Provence (base) → Valensole lavender & Moustiers-Sainte-Marie (Day 2) → Gordes, Roussillon & Luberon villages (Day 3) → Arles & the Camargue (Day 4) → either Marseille day trip or a second wine/village day (Day 5).

**Best time:** Mid-June through mid-July for lavender at peak bloom. Late August through September for smaller crowds and wine harvest energy, though lavender will be past its purple prime by then. May is cheaper, but lavender is typically still sparse and unreliable that early.

**A car is close to essential.** Public transport exists but runs at most a couple of times daily on the routes that matter for this itinerary. Trying to cover Valensole and Gordes without a car generally means relying on expensive taxis.

## Where to Base Yourself: Aix-en-Provence

Aix is the practical choice: it has a train station with [TGV connections to Paris and Marseille](https://www.sncf-connect.com), it's walkable, the markets are well-regarded, and it's centrally located for 1–2 hour drives to everywhere else on this itinerary without much backtracking.

The old town is touristy, especially around the fountain-lined Cours Mirabeau, but a few streets back from the main drag it quiets down considerably.

Good areas to stay: the Mazarine district or around Place Richelme — walkable to markets, restaurants, and the main sights without being in the busiest tourist zone. [Booking.com has options](https://www.booking.com/searchresults.html?ss=Aix-en-Provence) ranging from roughly €60–120/night in shoulder season (May, September) down to €45–60 in summer if booked early.

## Day 1: Easing In—Aix Markets & Old Town

After arriving by train, don't rush. Grab lunch at the market — Place Richelme or Place Madeleine — and pick up supplies: goat cheese wrapped in chestnut leaves (€6–8), olives (€4 for a bag), fresh pastries. A market-stand lunch runs €12–18.

Spend the afternoon walking. The Atelier de Cézanne is worth a visit for anyone interested in post-Impressionism (€7 entry), but simply wandering the cobbled streets and ducking into galleries and wine shops covers a lot of the Provence experience without checking specific boxes. Dinner around 8 PM at a small brasserie is a reasonable plan — most restaurants don't fill before then. Expect €20–30 for a main course plus a glass of rosé.

Sleep in Aix — there are early starts ahead.

## Day 2: Valensole Lavender & Moustiers-Sainte-Marie

An early start (around 6:30 AM) matters here: lavender in harsh midday light looks washed out, and early morning or late-afternoon golden hour are when the fields look their most vivid purple.

**Valensole to Aix is about 100 km (90 minutes).** This is rental car territory — pick one up at the Aix train station if you haven't already, expecting €45–65/day for a compact car in June. Fill up before heading to Valensole; fuel at small Provençal stations runs €1.70–1.85/liter, and prices spike at highway stops.

Plan on roughly 2 hours in the fields — not because that's how long it takes to see them, but because the light shifts and different angles are worth returning for. Bring a sun hat and water; there's no shade in the fields, and sunburn is a real risk.

**Then it's about 45 minutes to Moustiers-Sainte-Marie**, a postcard village perched above a gorge, well known for its pottery (faience). A late lunch at one of the village's terrace cafés runs roughly €14–20 for a salad and drink, and the ceramics shops are worth a look if the folk-art aesthetic appeals — though pieces are not cheap, typically €25–60 each.

Plan to be back in Aix by around 6 PM (90-minute drive), leaving time for dinner. Total driving for the day: roughly 240 km.

## Day 3: Gordes, Roussillon & The Luberon Villages

This is the "golden hour" day — the villages here tend to photograph beautifully in low light.

Leave Aix around 9 AM heading toward Gordes (90 km, 90 minutes). Gordes is a striking castle-crowned village cascading down a cliff, but it's typically busy with tour groups by 11 AM. Quick tip: park at the bottom and walk up — parking at the top (€3–4) fills fast.

An hour is usually enough for Gordes: the viewpoint by the castle, a coffee, some time absorbing the view. From there it's about 20 minutes southeast to Roussillon, a smaller village known for its ochre cliffs and noticeably fewer crowds, with a walkable main street and better odds of seeing locals rather than tour groups.

A bistro lunch in Roussillon runs roughly €16–24 for a main. The afternoon can be spent driving slowly through the Luberon villages: Ménerbes (on a ridge, tight uphill parking, but a striking setting), Lacoste (small and quiet), and Lourmarin (the largest and most commercial of the three). These towns sit 15–20 minutes apart by car.

It's not necessary to stop in all of them — picking two or three and lingering tends to work better than rushing through five and spending half the day parking.

Head back to Aix by around 6 PM, or stay overnight in Lourmarin if a more relaxed pace appeals — hotels there run roughly €80–140/night, and the drive back the next day is a straightforward 100 km.

## Day 4: Arles, The Camargue & Van Gogh Light

Arles is about 80 km south of Aix (90 minutes). Van Gogh lived here for roughly 15 months and painted prolifically during that time — worth knowing if art history is of interest, though the town is also simply a beautiful Roman settlement with good museums and notably good late-afternoon light regardless.

Start with the Roman amphitheater, the Arènes (€5 entry, or €8 combined with the ancient theater). Walk the narrow streets around the Place du Forum, where a café lunch overlooking the plaza runs roughly €18–26 for a salad and drink. The Van Gogh Foundation (€8–12) is worth a visit for anyone particularly interested, though simply standing in the plaza Van Gogh painted, and noticing how little the light has changed, is its own kind of experience.

**From there it's about 45 minutes into the Camargue**, a delta of marshes, salt flats, and wild horses — flat, sparse, and strikingly atmospheric. Salin-de-Giraud and the flamingo observation points are worth a stop; binoculars help. The detour takes roughly two hours total and breaks up the day well.

Plan to be back in Aix by around 7 PM (90-minute drive). Total driving for the day: roughly 200 km.

## Day 5: Marseille or A Second Wine Day

Two reasonable options here, depending on energy level by this point in the trip.

**Option A: Marseille day trip (80 km, 90 minutes south).**
Wander the Vieux Port, visit the Basilica of Notre-Dame de la Garde for the view (free if hiking up, roughly €10 for the cable car), and try bouillabaisse at a proper restaurant rather than a tourist-facing one (roughly €28–45). Marseille is grittier and less postcard-Provence than the rest of the itinerary, which is exactly its appeal for some travelers and less so for others after four days of villages.

**Option B: Stay in Provence for a second wine/village day.**
A guided winery tour runs roughly €45–60 per person, often including lunch and tastings, or it's straightforward to drive independently to Côtes de Provence wineries around Lorgues or Brignoles (about 60 km east). Most wineries allow drop-ins; tastings run €3–5 per person, and the fee is often waived with a bottle purchase. Expect €15–25 for a decent bottle.

If the pace has already been heavy, the wine day tends to be the more relaxing choice; Marseille suits travelers still wanting a change of scenery.

Sleep one more night in Aix before departing.

## Getting Around: Why a Car Matters

Provence's public transport is genuinely sparse for this route. The bus from Aix to Valensole, where it exists, may run only once a day and cost around €12 — fine if the timing works, a dead end if it doesn't. SNCF trains connect Aix to Arles and a few other towns, but the Luberon villages are effectively unreachable without a car.

Renting a car runs roughly €45–65/day for a manual compact (less if booked ahead) — around €225–325 over five days, well below the €400–600 a private driver would typically cost for the same routes. A car also buys flexibility: staying longer somewhere unexpectedly good, or leaving early if a stop disappoints.

## Budget: What 5 Days Actually Costs

A realistic breakdown for one person, mid-June pricing (shoulder-to-peak season):

- **Accommodation (4 nights in Aix):** €80–100/night = €320–400
- **Car rental (5 days):** €50–65/day = €250–325
- **Petrol (~500 km):** €85–100
- **Lavender field parking:** €5–8
- **Meals (breakfast, lunch, dinner):** €25–35/day = €125–175 (markets for breakfast/picnics bring this down)
- **Winery tastings/entries:** €20–40 total
- **Wine, if buying:** €30–60 (optional)
- **Miscellaneous (coffee, pastries, museum entries):** €40–50

**Total: roughly €875–1,250 for one person, or approximately €175–250 per day.**

Splitting the car and accommodation between two people brings this down to roughly €140–190 per person per day. Skipping wine tastings in favor of picnics can bring it closer to €150–200/day.

September tends to be cheaper across the board — hotels commonly drop to €60–80, and restaurant prices lose some of the peak-season premium. May is similar in cost, though lavender timing is unreliable that early.

## The Bottom Line

Five days in Provence means a lot of driving. Expect to see the highlights, eat well, and come home with good photos — but not to feel fully relaxed until day four or so. Seven days, if the schedule allows it, gives more breathing room: roughly three days in Aix and the Luberon, two in Arles and the Camargue, and two buffer days for the towns that turn out to deserve more than a couple of hours.

That said, five days works well enough to hit the highlights, taste the food, and catch the light. It won't feel like Provence has been fully "done" — but Provence rarely does; it's built to make people want to come back.

The lavender will still be there next year. So will the villages. So will the wine.
